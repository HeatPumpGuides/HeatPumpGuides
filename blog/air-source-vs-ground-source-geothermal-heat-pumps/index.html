Air Source vs. Ground Source (Geothermal) Heat Pumps

Ground source heat pumps are more efficient and last longer. Air source heat pumps are far cheaper to install and fit almost any home. Here's how they compare on efficiency, cost, lifespan and space.

The difference between air source and ground source heat pumps is where they get their heat. An air source heat pump pulls heat from the outdoor air. A ground source, or geothermal, heat pump pulls it from the ground. That one difference drives everything else: efficiency, cost, lifespan and how much space you need.

The short answer

  • Ground source is more efficient and lasts longer, but costs much more to install and needs yard space for a ground loop.
  • Air source is cheaper, installs quickly with no digging, and fits almost any home, but its efficiency varies with the weather.
  • Ground source makes most sense if you have land, cash up front, and plan to stay for decades.

How air source and ground source heat pumps differ

Every heat pump moves heat from one place to another instead of generating it. That's what makes heat pumps so efficient, as we explained in what a heat pump is and how it works. The two types differ only in where that heat comes from:

  • Air source heat pumps move heat between the outdoor air and your home.
  • Ground source (geothermal) heat pumps move heat between the ground and your home, through fluid circulating in buried pipes.

Efficiency

Efficiency is ground source's main advantage. A few feet down, the ground stays at about the same temperature all year, so a ground source heat pump always draws heat from a steady source. Its efficiency barely changes with the seasons.

Outdoor air is anything but steady. An air source heat pump typically runs at 300–400% efficiency, but on the coldest days at the bottom of its range that can fall to 100–200%. In dollar terms:

  • Air source: about $3 to $4 of heat for every $1 of electricity.
  • Ground source: about $5 to $6 of heat for every $1 of electricity.

For more on how efficiency changes with temperature, see our guide to COP, SCOP and HSPF.

Installation cost

Upfront cost is ground source's main drawback. It needs a ground loop, a series of U-shaped pipes buried in the yard, so the heat pump can use the earth as its heat source in winter and heat sink in summer. Installing one means digging trenches or drilling wells with heavy equipment and qualified operators, which adds a lot of time and money.

An air source install involves no digging. The contractor installs the heat pump, refrigerant lines, electrical wiring and, for a central system, ductwork.

Lifespan

Ground source systems last longer because most of their hardware is underground, protected from weather. The indoor heat pump typically lasts about 25 years, and the ground loop can last more than 50 years. An air source unit sits outdoors and wears faster. Most last 15 to 20 years, and up to 25 at the high end.

Space requirements

A ground loop needs room. With a small yard, or no yard, ground source may not be possible. An air source heat pump needs very little space: the outdoor unit mounts on the wall or on a pad beside the house.

Tax credits and incentives

Beyond federal programs, many states, cities and utilities offer rebates for both types. To find what's available where you live:

  • The Switch Is On lists rebates by zip code.
  • DSIRE, the Database of State Incentives for Renewables & Efficiency, is a comprehensive database of state and local programs.

Your state's page on Heat Pump Guides also lists local programs.

Editor's note · September 2026

When this episode was recorded in December 2024, two federal tax credits applied. The Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit covered 30% of an air source or ground source heat pump, up to $2,000. The Residential Clean Energy Credit covered 30% of a geothermal system with no cap, which made a big difference for ground source. Both credits were repealed and ended on December 31, 2025, so neither applies to installs from 2026 on. See the IRS pages for the Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit and the Residential Clean Energy Credit. Losing the uncapped geothermal credit makes ground source a harder sell on cost. State and utility incentives are separate and many are still available.

Which one is right for you?

Ground source makes sense if you:

  • have a large yard, or land for a ground loop;
  • have cash to invest up front; and
  • plan to stay in the home a long time, as in decades.

Over a long enough period, ground source's extra efficiency and longer lifespan can save more on energy bills and repairs than the higher installation cost.

Air source makes sense if you:

  • don't have the yard space for a ground loop;
  • want a lower upfront cost; or
  • don't expect to stay in the home long-term.

For most homeowners, especially since the federal geothermal credit ended, air source is the practical choice. To weigh a heat pump against your current system, see the pros and cons of heat pumps.
