Pros and Cons of Heat Pumps

Heat pumps are several times more efficient than a furnace, and they heat and cool in one unit. They also cost more to install and need to be sized right. Here are 9 pros and 9 cons for heating and cooling your home.

A heat pump can heat and cool your home at three to five times the efficiency of a gas furnace, with no combustion in the house. It also costs more to install, heats more slowly, and has to be sized carefully. Here are 9 pros and 9 cons of air source heat pumps for homes, the most common kind.

At a glance

Pros: energy efficiency, lower bills, heating and cooling in one unit, safety, lower emissions, dehumidification and comfort, cleaner air, incentives, no ducts needed.

Cons: higher install cost, lower efficiency in extreme cold, slower recovery, electrical upgrades, some noise, electricity prices, cooler supply air, insulation needs, harder to install well.

The pros of heat pumps

1. Energy efficiency

A gas furnace runs at about 80–95% efficiency. A heat pump runs at 300–500% or more, because it moves heat instead of making it. We explain how in what a heat pump is and how it works. According to the US Department of Energy's guide to home energy upgrades, a heat pump can cut the electricity used for heating by about 65% compared with electric resistance heat such as baseboard heaters.

2. Lower energy bills

Higher efficiency usually means lower bills, with one caveat: it depends on what electricity and your current fuel cost where you live. If you heat with cheap natural gas and pay a lot for electricity, the savings will be smaller. In most cases, though, a heat pump costs less to run. A DOE analysis found that for over 90% of US households, replacing worn-out heating equipment with the right heat pump would lower energy bills.

Don't forget cooling. A heat pump is also a very efficient air conditioner, so if you're replacing an old AC with a low SEER (seasonal energy efficiency ratio) rating, you'll save in summer too.

3. Heating and cooling in one unit

One heat pump replaces both a furnace and an air conditioner, which frees up space. Ductless mini-splits save even more, because there's no ductwork at all (see pro #9).

4. Safer: no combustion

Burning natural gas, propane or oil can produce carbon monoxide when combustion doesn't get enough oxygen. It's odorless, colorless and deadly at low concentrations, which is why homes with fuel-burning heat need CO detectors. Combustion also produces nitrogen oxides (NOx), which irritate the lungs and worsen asthma. That's why newer furnaces carry "low NOx" labels. A heat pump burns nothing, so neither is an issue.

5. Better for the environment

With no fossil fuel burned in the home, a heat pump cuts both local air pollution and climate-warming emissions. Even on a grid that still runs partly on fossil fuels, running a heat pump produces less CO2 than burning gas in a furnace. The numbers are in our heat pump myths episode.

6. Dehumidification and comfort

A heat pump works as a whole-home dehumidifier. Many have a dry mode that removes moisture even when the system isn't cooling, which helps a lot on muggy days. Most modern heat pumps also have variable-speed compressors. Instead of switching fully on and off like a furnace, they adjust output to match demand. That gives steady temperatures without the hot-then-cold swings of a system that cycles.

7. Cleaner indoor air

Besides removing combustion pollutants, heat pumps filter the air. Their filters catch dust, pollen, pet dander and mold spores. Because variable-speed systems run almost constantly, they're almost always filtering. Lower humidity also discourages mold and mildew. Some systems take high-efficiency filters or bring in fresh outside air.

8. Tax credits, rebates and incentives

Many states, cities and utilities offer rebates for heat pumps, because they cut emissions and improve indoor air. Some utilities also offer lower electricity rates for heat pump homes, like the discounted winter rates in Massachusetts. Your state's page on Heat Pump Guides lists local programs.

Editor's note · September 2026

When this episode aired in January 2025, a federal tax credit covered 30% of a heat pump installation, up to $2,000, and separate federal credits applied to insulation, windows and doors. Those credits (Section 25C) were repealed and ended on December 31, 2025. See the IRS page on the Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit. State, local and utility incentives are separate and many are still available.

9. No ductwork required

Almost every other central heating or cooling system needs ducts. Ductless mini-splits don't. That saves the energy a blower uses to push air through ducts. It also lets you put a unit in each room. You get room-by-room temperature control, so one person can keep their room cooler than another's, and redundancy: if one unit goes down, the rest of the house stays warm.

The cons of heat pumps

1. Higher installation cost

Costs vary a lot by equipment and region. An average central ducted heat pump install runs around $14,000, while a basic furnace or AC unit can cost under $5,000. A fair comparison sets the heat pump against a furnace plus an air conditioner. Even then, heat pumps usually cost more up front, which is why local rebates matter. For a cost comparison of heat pump types, see air source vs. ground source heat pumps.

2. Lower efficiency in very cold weather

Heat pumps don't stop working below freezing, especially cold-climate models, but their efficiency does drop as it gets colder. The unit we looked at in our COP, SCOP and HSPF episode is about twice as efficient at 47°F as at −13°F. It still heats in extreme cold, but costs more to run. That's why some homes in cold climates choose a dual-fuel system, a heat pump with a fossil fuel backup for the coldest days.

3. Longer recovery time

Recovery time is how long it takes to bring a cold house back up to temperature, for example from 50°F to 70°F after a vacation. A heat pump takes longer than a furnace. A smart thermostat solves most of this: start warming the house before you get home.

4. Higher electrical requirements

Heat pumps draw a lot of current. Most need a 20–30 amp breaker, and larger units up to 50 amps. An older home with a 100-amp panel may not have room. A panel upgrade can cost several thousand dollars, so check your panel and the unit's requirements early. Avoiding an upgrade can save a lot.

5. Some noise

The outdoor fan makes noise, typically about 40–50 decibels, similar to a quiet dishwasher. That's comparable to a traditional central AC. Most complaints come from older units that need maintenance. Some cities, like Seattle, have strict noise limits, especially at night, so make sure your contractor knows the local rules and checks the unit with a decibel meter.

6. Electricity can be expensive

Electricity can cost more than gas, especially in high-price areas like Massachusetts, Southern California and Hawaii, so savings aren't guaranteed. Look at your local rates and your coldest winter temperatures. Rooftop solar can offset this: many solar owners bank credits in summer and use them to run the heat pump in winter.

7. Cooler air from the vents

A heat pump typically delivers air at 85–92°F. A gas furnace delivers 120–140°F. Both heat the house, but furnace air feels noticeably hotter, and the gap is part of why heat pumps recover more slowly. In a leaky house, the cooler air can feel drafty. Newer models using R-290 (propane) refrigerant can reach supply temperatures close to a furnace's, but they aren't common yet.

8. You may need better insulation

Because a heat pump delivers lower-temperature air, it runs most economically in a house that holds its heat. Before installing, look at your insulation, doors and windows as well as your electrical panel. Insulation upgrades are often cheap, especially if you do them yourself. A properly sized heat pump will still work in a less-insulated home, as we explain in heat pump myths.

9. Harder to install well

Heat pumps need a more skilled installer, because sizing matters. Contractors often oversize gas furnaces on purpose to be safe on the coldest day. An oversized heat pump causes airflow problems, short-cycling and a shorter lifespan. Find a contractor who does a Manual J load calculation and uses Manual S to select equipment.
