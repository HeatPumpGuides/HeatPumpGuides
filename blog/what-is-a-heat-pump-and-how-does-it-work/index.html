What Is a Heat Pump and How Does It Work?

A heat pump is an air conditioner that also runs in reverse. Here's the simple physics of how it pulls heat out of freezing outdoor air, and why that makes it three to five times more efficient than a furnace.

A heat pump is an air conditioner that can also run in reverse. In summer it moves heat out of your house. In winter it moves heat from outside into your house, even when it's below freezing out. Because it moves heat rather than making it, a heat pump can be three to five times more efficient than the best gas furnace.

The short answer

  • What it is: a heating and cooling system that moves heat instead of burning fuel to make it.
  • How it heats in winter: it chills refrigerant below the outdoor temperature, so outdoor heat flows into it, then releases that heat indoors.
  • Why it's efficient: moving heat takes far less energy than creating it, so heat pumps reach 300–500% efficiency.

What is a heat pump?

The simplest description: a heat pump is an air conditioner that works in both directions. An air conditioner pumps heat from inside your house to the outside. A heat pump can do that, and it can also do the opposite, pumping heat from outside to inside to warm your home in winter.

That raises an obvious question. Heat always flows from hot to cold, never the other way. So if it's colder outside than inside, how can a heat pump move heat in? The answer starts with what "heat" and "cold" actually mean.

Temperature and heat, briefly

What temperature measures

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the molecules in a substance, or more simply, how fast they're moving. Zoom far enough into any material and you'd see its molecules vibrating and bumping into each other. Faster movement means a higher temperature. Slower movement means a lower one.

Absolute zero, and why "cold" is relative

If the molecules stopped moving entirely, the material would be as cold as anything can be. That's absolute zero: −273°C, or about −460°F (0 kelvin).

Measured against absolute zero, a freezing winter day at 32°F (0°C) is actually very warm. It's nearly 500°F above the coldest possible temperature. When we call something "cold," we really mean it has less heat, not none. Almost everything on Earth, including winter air, the ground and your hands, holds plenty of heat energy, because almost everything is far above absolute zero.

What heat is

Heat is energy that moves between two things at different temperatures, and it always moves from the warmer one to the colder one.

How a heat pump moves heat from cold air into a warm house

Put those ideas together and the trick becomes clear. To pull heat out of 32°F air, a heat pump just needs something colder than 32°F. Outdoor heat will then flow into it on its own.

That something is the refrigerant. Refrigerants have very low boiling points, so the heat pump can make its refrigerant far colder than the winter air. Here's the cycle:

  1. The heat pump sends very cold refrigerant through the outdoor coil.
  2. Because the refrigerant is colder than the outdoor air, heat flows from the air into the refrigerant.
  3. The heat pump carries that heat indoors and releases it into your home.
  4. The refrigerant goes back outside to collect more heat, and the cycle repeats.

You can watch this happen in the animated diagram on the Heat Pump Guides homepage. Switch it between heating and cooling mode.

The four main parts of a heat pump

Every heat pump has four main components that change the refrigerant's pressure and temperature so it can absorb heat in one place and release it in another:

  • Compressor: squeezes the refrigerant, raising its pressure and temperature.
  • Condenser: the coil where hot refrigerant releases its heat.
  • Expansion device: drops the refrigerant's pressure, making it very cold.
  • Evaporator: the coil where cold refrigerant absorbs heat.

Refrigerators and air conditioners use the same four parts. A heat pump adds one more: a reversing valve. It lets the refrigerant flow in either direction, which is what lets one machine both heat and cool.

Why heat pumps are so efficient

Any system that makes heat can at best turn all of its energy into heat, which is 100% efficiency. A heat pump doesn't make heat. It moves heat that's already there, so it can deliver several units of heat for each unit of electricity it uses.

Electric resistance heating, such as baseboard heaters, is already all-electric and 100% efficient, which sounds good until you compare it with a heat pump. For how efficiency is measured and how it changes in cold weather, see COP, SCOP and HSPF explained.

Why heat pumps are getting so much attention

Efficiency is one reason. The other is that heat pumps burn no fossil fuels. As all-electric heating that's far more efficient than the alternatives, they're central to cutting emissions from how we heat and cool buildings. Plenty of people still doubt they work in cold climates. We take that on in six common heat pump myths.

The main takeaway: there's heat energy all around us, in everything above absolute zero. Heat pumps use electricity and refrigerant to move that heat where you want it, instead of burning something to create it.
