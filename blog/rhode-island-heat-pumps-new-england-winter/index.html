Heat Pumps in Rhode Island: Surviving a New England Winter, Sizing It Right and Mistakes to Avoid

AJ from Vortechs HVAC in Wakefield explains when a heat pump can heat a Rhode Island home on its own, how to keep the outdoor unit above snow and ice, why a Manual J load calculation matters, and the three mistakes he warns every homeowner about.

A heat pump can heat a Rhode Island home through a New England winter, but whether it should be the only heat source depends on the house: its size, its insulation, and what heats it today. AJ from Vortechs HVAC, who has spent 12 years with the South County installer, explains how he decides, how to keep the outdoor unit working through snow and ice, and the three mistakes he warns every homeowner about.

The short answer

  • Heat pump only? Often yes, in a well-insulated home with a properly sized, cold-climate unit. Large pre-1900 houses need insulation, air sealing and often new windows first.
  • Snow and ice: mount the outdoor unit on a stand of at least 12 inches (18 inches or more where snow drifts), over crushed stone, with drainage for defrost water.
  • Sizing: insist on a Manual J load calculation, not a rule of thumb based on your old boiler.
  • Running cost: AJ ranks heat pumps cheapest to run, then natural gas, with oil and propane last.
  • Avoid: poor design, cheap equipment, and units installed where nobody can service them.

Meet the guest: AJ from Vortechs HVAC

Vortechs HVAC is based in Wakefield and calls itself the HVAC specialists of South County. The company has been in business for about 18 years and works on oil, gas, ducted and ductless systems as well as heat pumps. It began installing heat pumps in earnest around 2016, as the technology improved. AJ is entering his 12th year there and designs systems with homeowners. He also runs a heat pump alongside an oil boiler in his own house.

This is the third episode in our series of interviews with local heat pump contractors, and the first from New England. The earlier two were in Sacramento and Ukiah, California.

Can a heat pump heat a Rhode Island home on its own?

AJ's answer starts with two questions: how big is the house, and what heats it now? A common case in South County is a moderate-size home with a 20-year-old oil boiler, which was likely oversized to begin with. That house can often switch to heat pumps only, using one of the stronger cold-climate units (Mitsubishi's Hyper-Heat line, for example).

What he focuses on most is coverage: making sure every part of the house gets heat, so there are no hot and cold zones. A well-insulated house, or one that will be insulated through one of Rhode Island's programs, can be approached with a heat-pump-only mindset, as long as the system is sized for what the house needs and what the people living there are used to.

Older homes need work first

South County has many large homes built before 1900. AJ says heat pumps can heat them, but they need extra work first:

  • more insulation, especially in attics and basements
  • new windows, where the old ones are leaky
  • air sealing, or spray foam insulation, which makes the building envelope much tighter

In Rhode Island, the starting point for that work is Rhode Island Energy's no-cost Home Energy Assessment, carried out by RISE Engineering. The assessment identifies where your house loses heat and unlocks discounted insulation and air sealing. It's usually also the first step for heat pump rebates. The Rhode Island heat pump page lists the state's programs.

AJ adds that while South County winters are milder than northern New England or Canada, you should plan for the bad stretches. He recalls back-to-back storms last winter that buried the area under more than three feet of snow.

How to protect a heat pump from snow and ice

Snow is the obvious problem: an outdoor unit buried in a drift can't pull in air. Vortechs mounts outdoor units on 12- or 18-inch stands. Twelve inches is the standard minimum in the Northeast, but after last winter, more customers ask for 18 inches or higher. Some have even built small wooden platforms to lift their units clear of drifting snow.

The less obvious problem is ice. When a heat pump runs its defrost cycle to melt frost off the outdoor coil, the meltwater drips below the unit and can freeze into a block of ice. AJ's best-case setup is:

  1. A bed of crushed stone under the unit, so meltwater drains away instead of pooling
  2. A pad for a stable, level base
  3. A stand to hold the unit above the snow line

For waterfront homes, which are common in South County and catch a lot of wind, Vortechs adds extra drainage to direct the ice, and is considering wind guards on the outdoor fan so the motor keeps running in a heavy drift.

How to size a heat pump correctly

AJ works in phases. For a well-insulated house built in the last ten years, a quick block load based on square footage and perimeter gives a rough idea, especially when each floor is its own zone. To be precise, Vortechs runs a full Manual J load calculation, either in-house or through a third party. The ACCA Manual J method accounts for:

  • windows
  • insulation levels above and below the living space
  • the age of the roof
  • how the house is built, and what sits inside and outside the insulated envelope

The old approach, sizing a new system to match an oversized 30-year-old cast iron boiler, doesn't work for inverter heat pumps. As AJ puts it, the Manual J "gives me the answers to the test." From there he picks equipment. If a house needs 33,000 BTUs, that falls between a 30,000 and a 36,000 BTU unit. He also asks how the family likes the house to feel, because a system that's correct on paper still has to feel right to the people living there.

Is a blower door test worth it?

For tight houses, yes. A blower door test measures how airtight a house is. It matters most for new construction with spray foam insulation, where the house can end up so tight that it needs mechanical ventilation (an ERV or HRV) to bring in fresh air. In a house without enough makeup air, you can struggle to even open a window. AJ's advice applies whoever you hire: make sure your contractor understands what their design means for your house, because you'll be living with it.

Heat pump vs oil, gas and propane: running costs

This is the question AJ hears most. He can't give one number, because costs depend on the house and on how warm people keep it. A home kept at 60°F costs far less to heat than one kept at 74°F, whatever the fuel. But comparing like with like, at a steady 65°F, he ranks monthly costs like this:

Typical monthly heating cost, cheapest first, as AJ ranks them
RankHeat sourceWhy
1Inverter heat pumpThe most efficient option, even at a high electricity rate
2Natural gasPiped in and billed by use, with no tank to maintain
3Oil and propane (similar)Delivered tank fuels, marked up and tied to market prices

The caveat is electricity prices. A heat pump runs only on electricity, so the higher your rate, the smaller its lead. For one New England state's answer to that problem, see how Massachusetts cut electricity rates for heat pump owners.

AJ's own house shows how a partial switch can pay off. He has oil-fired hot water heat with a boiler and a single-zone Mitsubishi heat pump that heats his first floor. His winter electric bill now looks like his summer one, but with oil prices high, every gallon he doesn't burn is a saving, and he hasn't given up any comfort.

Three heat pump mistakes to avoid

  1. Bad design. Poor design will ruin any system, and it's unfair to blame the brand when the design is at fault.
  2. Cheap equipment. Brand matters. Don't expect a budget unit to perform like a top-of-the-line one.
  3. Units nobody can reach. Heat pumps need regular service and replacement parts over their lifetime. A unit squeezed into a crawlspace or tight attic may fit, but if a technician can't reach it, you'll pay for that later.

Which heat pump brands does AJ recommend?

Vortechs used to offer as many brands as possible. Years of service calls (tech support, parts lead times, how easy parts are to replace) narrowed that down to two:

  • Bosch for central (unitary) systems replacing central air, oil forced hot air or hydro-air. Its units cover a range of capacities, roughly 3 to 5 tons or 1 to 2.5 tons, so one model can fit a home's needs closely. AJ calls it an excellent drop-in replacement.
  • Mitsubishi for ducted and ductless mini-splits. It's the brand Vortechs installs most, and AJ says it outperforms Bosch at very low temperatures.

He also points to warranty length: both brands can offer warranties in the 10 to 12-year range, longer than some others.

Getting a heat pump quote in South County

AJ's last piece of advice is to learn before you buy. Research brands and understand how a heat pump works, so the conversation with your installer makes sense.
