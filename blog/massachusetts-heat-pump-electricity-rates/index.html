Cheaper Electricity Rates for Heat Pump Owners in Massachusetts

Massachusetts was the first state to cut winter electricity rates for homes that heat with a heat pump. Here's how the discount works, what it saved last winter, and why it's getting bigger.

Massachusetts is the first state to give homes that heat with a heat pump a lower electricity rate in winter. In the 2025–26 heating season, heat pump owners saved over $37 million in total, more than $250 per household. This winter the discount gets bigger.

This article covers how the seasonal heat pump rate works, how much it saves, who qualifies, and why it doesn't raise costs for everyone else. It also looks at the other states now considering the same idea.

The short answer

  • What it is: a lower winter electricity rate for homes heated with a heat pump, offered by Eversource, National Grid and Unitil.
  • When it applies: November 1 through April 30, on all of the household's electricity use.
  • Last winter: over $37 million saved across more than 140,000 households.
  • This winter: a further $16–$21 a month off for a typical home using 600 kWh.
  • Enrollment: automatic if you got a Mass Save heat pump rebate at your address since 2019.

What the Massachusetts heat pump rate is

State regulators worked with the state's three investor-owned electric utilities, National Grid, Eversource and Unitil, to create seasonal heat pump rates for residential customers. The rates started on November 1, 2025.

The discount doesn't touch the price of the electricity itself. It lowers the delivery part of the bill: the distribution charge and, for some utilities, the transmission charge. Those charges pay for the poles, wires and substations that carry power from the plant to your home.

Base distribution rate, winter 2025–26
UtilityHeat pump rateStandard rate (R-1)
Eversource2.05¢/kWh6.26¢/kWh
National Grid2.44¢/kWh6.68¢/kWh
Unitil3.63¢/kWh10.18¢/kWh

Source: Massachusetts Department of Public Utilities. The base distribution rate is one component of the total bill. Eversource heat pump customers also paid a lower winter transmission rate (1.49¢ versus 4.55¢ per kWh).

How much heat pump owners save

Last winter: $37 million

The first season ran from November 1, 2025 to the end of March 2026. Utilities cut rates by between 4.3 and 7.5 cents per kilowatt-hour compared with the standard winter price. More than 140,000 households saved about $37 million together, which works out to more than $250 per customer, according to Canary Media's analysis of the first winter of Massachusetts heat pump rates.

This winter: a bigger discount

From November 1, 2026, two utilities are also cutting the transmission charge for heat pump homes:

Winter transmission rate for heat pump customers
UtilityBeforeFrom Nov 1, 2026
National Grid heat pump rate5.5¢/kWh2.0¢/kWh
Unitil4.2¢/kWh1.5¢/kWh

A household using 600 kWh a month can expect its bill to drop by roughly $16 to $21 a month, on top of the savings it already got last winter. The details are in Canary Media's report on Massachusetts' bigger heat pump discounts.

Who qualifies, and how to sign up

You qualify if you are a residential customer of Eversource, National Grid or Unitil and you use a heat pump to help heat your home. The heat pump doesn't have to be your only heating system.

  • Automatic enrollment: the utilities enroll customers at any address that received a qualifying Mass Save heat pump rebate, or had a heat pump installed through Mass Save, since January 1, 2019.
  • Self-enrollment: if you installed a heat pump without a Mass Save rebate, contact your electric company to enroll.
  • Heat pump water heaters alone don't qualify. The heat pump has to heat the house.

The discount applies to all of the household's electricity use from November 1 to April 30, not just the heat pump's share. From May through October you pay the standard residential rate.

Does the discount raise costs for everyone else?

This is the obvious question: if heat pump owners pay less, does someone else have to pay more? For the most part, no. The reason is how the grid's costs work.

Poles, wires and substations are sized for peak demand, the single busiest time of the year. In Massachusetts today that peak comes in summer, on the hottest afternoons when everyone runs their air conditioning. Winter demand is much lower, so the extra electricity heat pumps use in winter mostly travels over capacity the grid already has. It costs the utility very little in upgrades or maintenance.

Before the discount, heat pump homes were paying far more toward that infrastructure than they cost to serve. In New York, for example, utilities spend only about 2% more to deliver power to a heat pump home, yet those households pay more than twice as much in delivery charges as neighbors who heat with fossil fuels. The Massachusetts rate corrects that imbalance, and the state says non-heat-pump customers won't pay more in winter because of it.

How long the discount is likely to last

The reasoning holds only while the grid peaks in summer. As more homes switch to heat pumps, winter demand will eventually overtake summer demand. ISO New England, which runs the regional grid, expects that shift within about 10 years. Once it happens, winter rates will need to be revisited, and this discount will probably shrink or end. For the next several winters, though, it is expected to stay and may even grow.

Why it matters: heat pumps vs. natural gas

The main aim of the rate is to make heat pumps cheaper to run than natural gas, the most common heating fuel in Massachusetts and the country. A heat pump is several times more efficient than a gas furnace. Even so, high electricity prices can make it more expensive to run, a drawback we cover in the pros and cons of heat pumps.

The discounted rates change that math. With the latest cuts, more than half of Massachusetts households would save money by switching from natural gas heat to a heat pump. Recent increases in heating oil prices could push more oil-heated homes to switch as well. You can see how common heat pumps already are, along with local incentives, on our Massachusetts heat pump page.

Other states considering heat pump rates

Massachusetts was first, but others are moving in the same direction:

  • Minnesota: Xcel Energy has an electric heating rate.
  • Maine: Central Maine Power recently finished a pilot program.
  • New York and Rhode Island: both are considering similar rates. Rhode Island has proposed a cut of 5.8 cents per kWh.

For a bigger picture of where heat pumps are common and where they're rare, see which US states have the most heat pumps.
