So on the first episode of this podcast we covered the basic physics behind how heat pumps work and the main takeaway was that heat pumps transfer heat from one place to another rather than generating it which is how they are able to operate at such high efficiencies. The main difference between air source heat pumps and ground source heat pumps is where they're getting that heat from. As their names would suggest air source heat pumps transfer heat from the outside air into your home and ground source heat pumps transfer heat from the ground into your home. The main advantage of ground source heat pumps over air source heat pumps is efficiency. Since groundwater is insulated by the earth groundwater temperatures are basically the same year-round which means that ground source heat pumps always have a heat source to pull from that is at a constant temperature.
[0:51] Outside air temperature however unlike groundwater temperature is not constant year-round. This is why the efficiency of air source heat pumps can vary a lot from 300 to 400% efficiency which is typical all the way down to 100 to 200% at the very coldest temperatures of its operating range. In other words for every dollar of electricity you put into an air source heat pump you can expect to get three to four dollars of heating out of it and for every dollar of electricity you put into a ground source heat pump you can get five to six dollars of heating out of it. And the efficiency of a ground source heat pump is basically the same year-round whereas the efficiency of an air source heat pump can vary widely based on the weather. The other big difference between air source and ground source heat pumps is upfront cost. Ground source heat pumps are typically more expensive to install since they require putting in a ground loop. This is a series of u-shaped tubes that are buried underground so that the heat pump can use the earth as a heat source or heat sink. Installing this ground loop requires digging a trench which involves heavy machinery like excavators and contractors that are qualified to use them.
[1:45] This greatly increases the time and the cost of the install. With air source heat pump installs there is no digging involved at all and the contractors only have to put in the heat pump itself some refrigerant lines and electrical cables and maybe some ducting if it's a central unit. As of the time of making this podcast air source heat pump installs can run you about $3,500 to $6,000 for a ductless system install and between $12,000 and $20,000 on the high end for a central ducted system. Ground source heat pump installs on the other hand run from about $10,000 to $30,000 and the cost can get up to $50,000 or more depending on the trenching and wells that you dig. Another consideration when comparing the two is lifespan. Ground source heat pumps typically have a longer lifespan than air source heat pumps because their main components are located underground and that means that they're protected from the weather and other outdoor disturbances.
[2:36] Air source heat pumps on the other hand are more exposed to the elements and therefore they degrade at a quicker rate. Most air source heat pumps will last you about 15 to 20 years maybe 25 years on the higher end and ground source heat pumps will last 25 years typically while the ground loop itself can last more than 50 years. Another consideration to keep in mind when comparing the two is the space requirement. As we mentioned before with ground source heat pumps you have to install a ground loop which involves digging a trench installing piping underground so if you don't have a very large yard or you don't have a yard at all the ground source heat pump might not be doable. Air source heat pumps on the other hand don't have much of a space requirement at all. The heat pump and all of its associated components are located above ground and can be mounted right to your house or on a platform that's right alongside your house.
