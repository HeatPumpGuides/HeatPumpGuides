The short answer

  • City of Ukiah electric customers: a per-ton rebate, about $200 a ton, so roughly $800 for a 4-ton system.
  • Sonoma Clean Power customers (PG&E territory in Sonoma and Mendocino counties): a flat $1,000 rebate for a qualifying heat pump.
  • Winter: a regular heat pump keeps up with Ukiah winters without a gas backup. Cold-climate models and backup strip heat are options, not requirements.
  • Ducted or ductless: keep your ducts if they're in good shape. Go ductless if they've had water or rodent problems.
  • Solar: a heat pump moves heating onto your electric bill, which solar panels can offset.

Heat pump rebates in Ukiah and Mendocino County

Which rebate you can get depends on your electric provider. Ukiah runs its own municipal electric utility. Most homes outside city limits are PG&E customers, and in Sonoma and Mendocino counties that usually means their electricity is supplied through Sonoma Clean Power.

Seb put the Ukiah rebate at $175 or $200 per ton. The city's rebate administrator currently lists $200 per ton for qualifying systems; check the City of Ukiah Electric Utility rebate programs for the current amount. Sonoma Clean Power's terms are on its heat pump heating and cooling rebate page.

A few details on the Sonoma Clean Power rebate are worth knowing before you buy:

  • The installation address has to be on a Sonoma Clean Power account.
  • Dual fuel systems don't qualify. A heat pump paired with a gas furnace backup won't get the rebate.
  • You have to apply within 90 days of the install.
  • Income-qualified households on CARE or FERA can get a much larger rebate, covering half the remaining project cost up to $10,000.

Seb added that other rebates appear from time to time as funding becomes available. Statewide, the TECH Clean California single-family incentives and the federal heat pump tax credit have both ended, as we covered in the Sacramento episode. The California heat pump page tracks which statewide programs are open.

Heat pump vs gas or propane: what it costs to run

Seb says it's hard to give one number for Ukiah, because two things vary a lot from home to home:

  1. What you heat with now. In town it's usually natural gas. On the rural outskirts, propane is common, and AC&R still sees some much older diesel and kerosene systems.
  2. Who you buy electricity from. According to Seb, City of Ukiah electric rates are a lot lower per kilowatt-hour than PG&E's, which makes a heat pump cheaper to run inside city limits.

For cooling, a heat pump costs less to run than most traditional air conditioners, because it's more efficient. For heating, he says heat pumps are typically cheaper to run than a gas furnace, as long as you use them the right way. A heat pump works best when you set a temperature and let it hold it. A furnace gets used in short bursts: you feel cold, turn it on, and it fires up quickly. Running a heat pump the same way wastes much of its efficiency advantage.

Seb mentioned that some places no longer allow new gas appliances. There's no statewide ban in California. Can a heat pump handle a Northern California winter?

Yes. In Ukiah, Seb says a regular heat pump has never been a problem, including in his own house. On very cold mornings, frost can build up on the outdoor coil, and the system switches to a short defrost mode to clear it. You might lose a little heat for a few minutes while that happens. He says he has never noticed it at home, and most customers don't either.

If your home is somewhere colder, or you want extra margin on the coldest mornings, there are two options:

  • Electric strip heat: a backup electric heating element added alongside the heat pump.
  • A cold-climate (Hyper-Heat) model: a heat pump built to keep its heating output up as temperatures drop.

For most of Northern California, though, a standard heat pump works fine. The idea that heat pumps can't handle the cold is one of the most common heat pump myths.

Ducted heat pump or ductless mini-split?

Seb's advice comes down to the condition of your ductwork:

  • Ducts in good shape: a ducted heat pump is usually the less expensive switch, because it reuses what you already have.
  • Ducts with a history of trouble: if the ducts run under the house and that space has flooded, or rodents have gotten into the attic or crawlspace, going ductless avoids the problem entirely.

As for efficiency, he says both are very efficient as long as the ductwork is good. Keep the SEER2 minimums in the table above in mind, too: Sonoma Clean Power sets a higher bar for single-zone ductless systems than for ducted ones.

Do heat pumps pair well with solar panels?

Very well, according to Seb. AC&R has installed solar and battery backup systems since 2009. When you replace a gas furnace with a heat pump, your heating moves onto your electric bill. Solar panels offset that new electricity use, and the more of your home's appliances you switch to electric, the more of your energy use solar can cover.

What to expect after switching from a gas furnace

Asked about common install problems, Seb didn't have any to warn about. The only feedback AC&R occasionally hears is that the heat feels different. A heat pump doesn't hit you with hot air right away the way a furnace does. It warms the house more gradually and keeps it there. Otherwise, AC&R now installs more heat pumps than traditional AC and furnace systems, and Seb says customers are generally very happy with them.

It's also worth asking about the warranty. AC&R is a Mitsubishi Diamond Elite contractor, which lets it offer a 12-year warranty on its installations. Mitsubishi's Diamond Contractor program extends the warranty when one of its certified contractors installs and registers the system.
