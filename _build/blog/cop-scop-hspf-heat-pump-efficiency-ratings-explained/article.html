COP, SCOP and HSPF are the three numbers that tell you how efficiently a heat pump heats. COP is efficiency at a single moment. SCOP and HSPF are efficiency over a whole heating season, measured in different units. Here's how each works and how to use them when choosing a heat pump.

Quick definitions

  • COP (coefficient of performance): heat out ÷ electricity in, at a given moment. A COP of 3 means 300% efficiency.
  • SCOP (seasonal coefficient of performance): the average COP over a whole heating season. Unitless (kW ÷ kW).
  • HSPF (heating seasonal performance factor): the same seasonal idea in BTUs per watt-hour. HSPF = SCOP × 3.412.

What is COP?

For heat pumps, COP stands for coefficient of performance. (It can also mean "chief of police," or the UN's annual climate conference, the Conference of the Parties, but not here.) COP is the most basic measure of how efficiently a heat pump heats.

How COP is calculated

COP is the ratio of heat output to electrical input:

COP = heat output ÷ electrical power input

If a heat pump uses 1 kilowatt of electricity to deliver 3 kilowatts of heat, its COP is 3 ÷ 1 = 3. That's the same as 300% efficiency. Technically, the heat output is what the condenser releases into your home, and the power input is what runs the compressor.

A heat pump can beat 100% because it moves existing heat rather than making new heat. We cover the physics in what a heat pump is and how it works.

Typical COP values

  • Air source heat pumps: about 3 to 4 in typical conditions.
  • Newer cold-climate models: some exceed 5 in mild weather.
  • Ground source (geothermal) heat pumps: about 6 to 7, because ground temperature stays steady. See air source vs. ground source heat pumps.

Why COP depends on outdoor temperature

An air source heat pump's COP changes a lot with the weather. The colder it is outside, the less heat there is in the air to collect, and the harder the heat pump works to deliver the same warmth.

This unit is more than twice as efficient at 47°F as at −13°F. It's still over 200% efficient at −13°F, which is why "heat pumps don't work in the cold" is a myth.

How to read a spec sheet

Manufacturers often advertise COP at a fairly mild temperature, which makes a unit look very efficient. Before you buy, open the full spec sheet and compare it with your own winters:

  1. Check the operating range. Can the unit run at the lowest temperatures you get?
  2. Check COP at those temperatures. How efficient is it on your coldest days, not just on mild ones?

What is SCOP?

Because COP moves so much with temperature, a single COP figure doesn't tell you how a heat pump performs over a whole winter. SCOP, the seasonal coefficient of performance, does. It's the heat pump's average efficiency across the entire heating season.

SCOP depends on location. The same heat pump will post a much higher SCOP through a South Carolina winter than through a northern Maine winter. That's why there are standard methods for calculating it. In Europe, the method is set by EN 14825. In outline:

  1. Identify the climate zone the heat pump will operate in.
  2. Get the heat pump's performance data at a range of temperatures.
  3. Weight each temperature by how often that climate sees it across the heating season.
  4. Calculate the weighted average COP.

The colder your winters, the lower the SCOP for any given heat pump.

What is HSPF?

HSPF, the heating seasonal performance factor, measures the same thing as SCOP, seasonal heating efficiency, in different units. It's the standard rating in the United States.

  • SCOP is unitless: kilowatts of heat per kilowatt of electricity.
  • HSPF uses BTUs of heat per watt-hour of electricity.

How to convert HSPF to SCOP

One watt-hour equals 3.412 BTUs, so:

  • HSPF = SCOP × 3.412
  • SCOP = HSPF ÷ 3.412

For example, the Mitsubishi unit above has an HSPF of 12.5, which is a seasonal COP of about 3.7.

A note on HSPF2

Since January 2023, US ratings have used an updated test procedure called HSPF2. Its test conditions are closer to real installations, so the same heat pump gets a lower HSPF2 number than its old HSPF rating. When you compare models, compare HSPF2 with HSPF2, not with an older HSPF figure.
