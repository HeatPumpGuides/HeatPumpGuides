California Senate Bill 222 is meant to make installing a heat pump faster and cheaper. It speeds up permits and inspections, caps what cities can charge for permits, and stops HOAs from banning heat pumps. It's one of the rare bills in which government tries to get out of its own way.

Editor's note · November 2026

This episode was recorded in February 2026, when SB 222 had cleared the Senate. It is now law. The bill, renamed the Heat Pump Access Act, passed the full Legislature on August 25, 2026: 26–5 in the Senate and 60–8 in the Assembly. Governor Newsom signed it, and it was chaptered on September 28, 2026 as Chapter 738, Statutes of 2026. The final version sets firm dates: asynchronous inspections by July 1, 2027, and online automated permitting by July 1, 2028. It also requires contractors to certify that they did a load calculation to size the equipment. Details below describe the bill as it stood when we recorded, and some provisions changed in amendments before it passed.

What SB 222 does

  • Faster permits and inspections: online permitting for simple home heat pump installs, and inspections that don't require the contractor to be on site.
  • Lower permit fees: fees limited to what the permit actually costs the city to process.
  • No HOA bans: homeowners associations can't prohibit heat pumps, or prohibit replacing a gas appliance with an electric one.
  • Local control kept: cities still enforce energy codes and can require separate permits for panel upgrades or major structural work.

Why California wants to speed up heat pump installs

In 2022, California set a goal of 6 million heat pumps installed by 2030. The state is behind: current projections put it closer to 4 million by 2030. Legislators looked at those numbers and decided something had to change.

Two big things are slowing heat pump adoption in California and elsewhere:

  1. High electricity prices, which make heat pumps more expensive to run. That's a topic for another episode. For one state's answer, see how Massachusetts cut electricity rates for heat pump owners.
  2. Local permitting and inspection delays, which add time and cost to every install. This is what SB 222 is aimed at.

The UC Berkeley Center for Law, Energy and the Environment report on heat pump retrofits covers the barriers in more depth.

What SB 222 would change

1. Faster permits and inspections

Cities and counties would have to allow faster, more flexible inspections, including ones where the contractor doesn't need to be there in person unless there's a problem. They would also have to offer instant online permits for straightforward residential heat pump installs. Permit applications and documents would need to be available and submittable online.

2. Less red tape and lower fees

Local governments couldn't charge excessive permit fees for heat pumps. Fees would generally be capped at the city's actual cost of processing the permit, meaning its staff time and administrative costs. As introduced, the bill also set a hard $150 cap on permits for heat pump water heaters. Plug-in window heat pumps and AC units running on 120 volts or less wouldn't need a permit or inspection at all.

3. HOAs can't block electrification

Some homeowners associations ban outdoor heat pump units over looks or noise. SB 222 would make those bans unenforceable statewide. It goes further: HOAs couldn't prohibit replacing any gas appliance with an electric one.

4. Cities keep some control

The bill doesn't take over local building departments. Cities and counties can still enforce building efficiency rules and local energy codes. They can still require separate permits for electrical panel upgrades, demolition, or significant structural work.

Enforcement runs through funding. Local governments that want certain energy funding from the California Energy Commission would have to follow these rules.

Why the earlier version, SB 282, failed

SB 222 is the second attempt. An earlier bill, SB 282, was introduced in 2025, made it through its first committee review, and then died in February 2026.

The likely reason is that SB 282 pushed too hard against local governments. It would have created:

  • a statewide contractor certification and training program, which brought new cost and bureaucracy, and uncertainty for the industry about what it would require; and
  • a statewide permitting program, which would have taken permitting power away from cities and counties.

Permitting and building codes are a long-running point of friction between Sacramento and local governments, and local groups lobby hard on them. SB 222 avoids that fight. It leaves permitting with cities and counties and drops the statewide certification program. It focuses instead on faster timelines, online applications, fee limits and flexible inspections. For a closer look at how the two bills compare, see Canary Media's coverage of California's heat pump and balcony solar bills.

Where SB 222 stands

When we recorded in February 2026, SB 222 had passed its first three stages, first committee review and the Senate floor, with the last vote on January 26, 2026. It still needed to pass Assembly committee review and an Assembly floor vote, and then be signed into law.

It has since passed the Legislature and was signed into law on September 28, 2026 (see the editor's note above). The bill's author is Senator Scott Wiener, with co-authors Senators Benjamin Allen, Josh Becker and Henry Stern.

What you can do

Our challenge to listeners: go to a local city or town meeting, or call your state representative, and ask them to cut red tape for electrification. That covers heat pumps, heat pump water heaters, solar panels and batteries. It may not feel like much, but those small actions add up.
