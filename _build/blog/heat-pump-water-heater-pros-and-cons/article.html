A heat pump water heater is the most efficient way to heat water, by a wide margin. It uses roughly a third to a quarter of the energy of a conventional electric water heater. It also costs more up front, heats water more slowly, and needs more space. Below are 9 pros and 9 cons, so you can decide whether one makes sense for your home.

What is a heat pump water heater?

A heat pump water heater uses a heat pump to heat water. It doesn't make heat by burning gas or propane, or by running current through an electric heating element. It moves heat that's already in the surrounding air into the water.

The easiest way to picture it is as a refrigerator in reverse. A fridge pulls heat out of its insides and dumps it into your kitchen. A heat pump water heater pulls heat out of the room it's in and puts it into the water tank. The principle is the same one behind every heat pump, which we explain in what a heat pump is and how it works.

Pros: efficiency, lower bills, safety and air quality, lower emissions, free cooling, free dehumidification, long warranties, energy independence, rebates.

The pros of heat pump water heaters

1. Much higher energy efficiency

Water heater efficiency is measured by Uniform Energy Factor (UEF), sometimes shortened to energy factor. It compares the energy that ends up in the water with the energy the heater uses. Higher is better.

Typical Uniform Energy Factor by water heater type
TypeUEF
Gas (tank and tankless)0.63 – 0.95
Electric resistance0.93 – 0.95
Heat pump3.3 – 4.1

A heat pump water heater is three to four times as efficient as any other kind. Tankless gas models sit at the top of the gas range.

2. Lower energy bills

According to ENERGY STAR, a certified heat pump water heater can save a household of four about $550 a year compared with a standard electric water heater, and more than $5,600 over its lifetime. Your savings depend on local electricity and gas prices. Because the efficiency gap is so large, though, almost everyone saves something. See ENERGY STAR's heat pump water heater savings estimates for the assumptions.

3. Safer, with better indoor air quality

Burning natural gas or propane carries a small risk of producing carbon monoxide if combustion doesn't get enough oxygen. Carbon monoxide is odorless, colorless and deadly even in small amounts, which is why homes with gas appliances need CO detectors. Combustion also produces nitrogen oxides (NOx), which can worsen asthma and other breathing problems. Even "low NOx" and "ultra-low NOx" appliances still produce some. A heat pump water heater burns nothing, so it produces neither.

4. Lower emissions

A heat pump water heater burns no fossil fuel on site and uses a fraction of the electricity of a resistance model. Even on a grid that still burns fossil fuels, it produces fewer emissions than a gas water heater. We run the numbers in our heat pump myths episode.

5. Free cooling

Because it pulls heat out of the air around it, a heat pump water heater noticeably cools the space it sits in, whether that's a garage, basement or utility room. In hot climates like Florida, that's cooling you don't have to pay an air conditioner for.

6. Free dehumidification

Pulling heat out of the air also pulls out moisture, so the unit works as a dehumidifier too. That's a real benefit in humid climates. If you'd rather cool and dry a different room, many units let you duct the air intake and exhaust to another space, such as the kitchen.

7. Longer warranties

Most heat pump water heaters come with longer warranties than conventional tanks. Owners have reported problems like refrigerant leaks in year nine and received a free replacement under warranty. Check the warranty terms when you compare models.

8. Energy independence

You can make your own electricity, but you can't make your own natural gas. With rooftop solar and a home battery, an all-electric home can keep running when the grid is down. A gas outage leaves a gas water heater with nothing to burn. Gas networks are very reliable, but outages do happen, especially as natural disasters become more frequent.

9. Rebates and incentives

Many states, cities and utilities offer rebates for heat pump water heaters. Massachusetts, New York and Oregon all have programs, and California's TECH Clean California offered instant rebates. You can look up what's available near you with the Switch Is On incentive finder or ENERGY STAR's rebate finder.

The cons of heat pump water heaters

1. Higher upfront cost

When we recorded in January 2025, Home Depot listed comparable 50-gallon Rheem models at these prices:

50-gallon Rheem water heaters, January 2025
TypePrice
Natural gas$609
Electric resistance$619
Hybrid heat pump$1,580

That's more than double the price, before installation. Prices change, so treat these as a rough comparison. Rebates and lower bills can make up the difference over time.

2. Noise

The heat pump on top has a fan and compressor, and it typically runs at about 45 to 60 decibels. The low end is like a refrigerator's hum or quiet conversation. The high end is noticeable, especially at peak output or in colder months. Think about noise if the tank sits next to a bedroom or living space.

3. Slower recovery time

Recovery time is how long the tank takes to heat back up after you use the hot water. A typical 50-gallon gas tank recovers in about 45 minutes. A heat pump water heater can take an hour to several hours, depending on how cold the surrounding air is.

The fix is hybrid mode. The unit uses the efficient heat pump most of the time and switches on its backup resistance elements when demand spikes, such as the whole family showering back to back. Most owners leave their units in hybrid mode for that reason.

4. Electrical requirements

Most heat pump water heaters need a 240-volt circuit. In US homes, most outlets are 120 volts, and 240-volt circuits usually serve only the dryer and maybe an EV charger. If there's no 240-volt circuit near your water heater, adding one raises the install cost. There are 120-volt models that plug into a standard outlet, which can be a good fit when running a new circuit is hard.

5. Space requirements

Height

A heat pump water heater is a tank with a heat pump on top, so it's taller. The 50-gallon Rheem heat pump model is 63.6 inches tall, versus 48 inches for the same-size Rheem electric tank. That's almost 16 inches more, so measure the space under a low ceiling before you buy.

Air volume

The unit needs enough air to pull heat from. Manufacturers typically recommend about 700 cubic feet of open space for a 50-gallon unit, roughly an 8×8-foot room with 10-foot ceilings. In a smaller space you may need to duct air in from another room or from outside.

6. A condensate drain is required

The moisture the unit pulls out of the air has to go somewhere. A heat pump water heater needs a condensate drain line to a floor drain or outside, and sometimes a small condensate pump. That's an extra piece of plumbing a conventional water heater doesn't need.

7. Performance in cold spaces

Heat pump water heaters work best with surrounding air between about 40°F and 90°F. In a cold garage, basement or outdoor installation in a cold climate, efficiency drops and the unit may struggle. Even then it's usually more efficient than a gas or resistance heater, but it matters where the tank goes.

8. Unwanted cooling and drying

The cooling and dehumidifying that count as pros in Florida or South Carolina can be a con somewhere cold and dry. The cooling effect is significant. A basement that's already chilly in winter will get noticeably colder, which you may not want.

9. It can be hard to find an installer

Plumbers usually install and service water heaters, but a heat pump water heater contains refrigerant. Working on refrigerant requires EPA refrigerant certification, which HVAC technicians usually have and plumbing-only companies often don't. Some homeowners struggle to find anyone willing to install or repair one. Look for companies that do both plumbing and HVAC, or ask us to connect you with an installer. Is a heat pump water heater worth it?

For most homes, yes. The efficiency gain is large enough that lower bills, and rebates where they're available, usually pay back the higher price over the life of the unit. It's the best fit when you have:

  • enough space and air around the tank, and the height to fit it;
  • a 240-volt circuit nearby, or you choose a 120-volt model;
  • a spot where extra cooling and drying are welcome, or can be ducted elsewhere;
  • an installer who's comfortable with refrigerant.
