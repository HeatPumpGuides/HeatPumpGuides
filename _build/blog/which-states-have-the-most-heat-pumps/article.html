South Carolina has the highest heat pump adoption rate in the United States: 41.7% of homes there use a heat pump as their main source of heat. Florida has the most heat pumps overall, at about 2.6 million households. At the other end, Alaska and Hawaii are tied for last at 0.2%.

You can rank states by heat pumps in two ways, and the two lists come out quite differently. Below are both, followed by why the South is so far ahead and where the numbers come from.

The short answer

  • Highest adoption rate: South Carolina (41.7%), then Alabama, North Carolina, Tennessee and Florida.
  • Most heat pumps in total: Florida (2.6 million households), then Texas, North Carolina and Georgia.
  • Lowest adoption rate: Alaska and Hawaii (0.2% each), then Wyoming, Wisconsin and Colorado.
  • Nationally: about 14% of US homes heated mainly with a heat pump in 2020.

Two ways to measure heat pump use by state

The first measure is the total number of homes in a state that heat with a heat pump. The second is the adoption rate, meaning the share of all homes in the state that use one.

The adoption rate is the better comparison. A total count mostly tracks population. Big states have more homes, so they have more of everything, heat pumps included. The adoption rate shows how common heat pumps actually are where you live.

Why southern states lead on heat pumps

The top of the list is all in the South, and the bottom is mostly in cold northern states. Three things account for most of that.

Mild winters and hot summers

In the South, nearly every home needs air conditioning for the summer. A heat pump is essentially a two-way air conditioner. It moves heat out of the house in summer, and in winter it runs in reverse to bring heat in. So if you're buying an AC unit anyway, it makes sense to buy one that can also heat the house.

Winters that are easy on older equipment

Southern winters are mild, so heating doesn't ask much of a heat pump. Even units that are 10 to 20 years old handle it without trouble. That reliability has helped heat pumps become the normal choice in the region over decades.

Why cold states lag behind

Colorado, Wisconsin, Wyoming and Alaska are strongly tied to fossil fuel heating, mostly natural gas, propane and heating oil. Cold-climate heat pumps have improved a lot (the idea that heat pumps don't work in the cold is now a myth), but they haven't caught on in these states yet. Hawaii is a different case: it ranks near the bottom because most homes there barely need heating at all.

Where the numbers come from

Every state figure above comes from the US Energy Information Administration's Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) 2020 state data. RECS is a large national survey of how American homes use energy, run every few years. The state totals match the Atlas Buildings Hub residential heat pump market update, which analyzes the same survey. Canary Media's chart of which states have the most heat pumps shows a similar picture.

How current is this data?

The survey was taken in 2020, so the numbers are about six years old. EIA ran the survey again in 2024, but it has released only part of the results so far. The space heating data, where the heat pump figures come from, is scheduled for spring 2027. When it comes out, we'll update these rankings. We expect adoption to have risen in many states, especially the ones now pushing hard to speed up heat pump installations.

Are ductless mini-splits counted?

Yes. The state adoption figures here count homes whose main heating system is either a central (ducted) heat pump or a ductless mini-split. The number matters in the Northeast, where mini-splits are common. EIA's own summary table counts central heat pumps only, so it undercounts those states. Each state page on this site explains the method in more detail, including how many homes run a mini-split alongside another heating system.

The national picture

Across the whole country, about 14% of homes (roughly 17.2 million households) heated primarily with a heat pump in 2020, up from 8% in 2005. On the episode we put the figure at around 16% today, or about 23 million heat pumps in total. That's an estimate, since the official survey data for recent years isn't out yet. Either way, most American homes still heat with something else, which leaves plenty of room to grow.
