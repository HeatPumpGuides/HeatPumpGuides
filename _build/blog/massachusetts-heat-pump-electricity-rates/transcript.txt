[0:00] Hello everybody, welcome back to the Heat Pump Guides podcast. Today we are going to be talking about the beautiful, amazing state of Massachusetts, which, fun fact, is where I'm from. And the reason we're going to be talking about Massachusetts today is that it is the first state in the country to have passed landmark legislation to make electricity rates cheaper for homeowners that heat their homes with heat pumps. Last heating season, from November 1st, 2025 to March 31st of this year, heat pump owners in Massachusetts saved over $37 million in total thanks to these discounted rates, which is over $250 per customer. And just recently, news broke that these electricity rates will be discounted even further this coming winter.
[0:40] And the way this discount actually works is that the state regulators worked with the three major utilities, National Grid, Eversource, and Unitil, to lower the delivery portion of customers' electric bills, which is the portion that covers transmission and distribution. As for the actual numbers of the discount, last heating season, rates were discounted by about 7.5 cents per kilowatt hour. And this coming winter, National Grid will drop their transmission charges from 5.5 cents per kilowatt hour down to 2 cents. And Unitil will lower their transmission rate from 4.2 cents down to 1.5 cents per kilowatt hour. So for households that use 600 kilowatt hours of power a month, they can expect their bill to drop by about $16 to $21 a month. And this is in addition to the savings that were already there from last winter.
[1:25] Now, these discounted electricity rates have caused some people to ask the question of fairness. Doesn't having heat pump owners with cheap electricity shift the cost to homeowners that still heat with fossil fuels? And the cool part is the answer is not really, since the discount, again, is only for the transmission and distribution part of electric bills, which together is known as the delivery portion. And this is the part that covers the cost of the physical infrastructure of the grid, so the poles and the wires that transport the electricity from the power plants to homes. And the cost of this infrastructure is largely based on peak electricity demand, which, as of right now, is always in the summer, since this is when everyone is running their air conditioning units on the hottest days of the year. So when heat pumps are running in the winter, when electricity demand is much lower, it doesn't really cost utilities much in terms of maintenance or upgrades, but these homeowners are still paying more.
[2:14] For example, in New York, it costs utilities just 2% more to deliver energy to heat pump users than to other homes, but those heat pump households pay more than twice as much in delivery charges as their neighbors that heat with fossil fuels. So that's the cool part about this discount, not only is it beneficial for accelerating electrification, but it's also making the cost structure more fair overall. Now, eventually, as more heat pumps get installed, the peak electricity demand will happen in the winter instead of the summer, and when that happens, these rates will have to be readjusted. The grid operator for New England predicts the region will shift to winter peaks within the next 10 years, so when that happens, this discount will likely come to an end. But for this year and the coming few years, we can expect this discount to remain and maybe even get bigger.
[2:59] A big reason for this discount is to make heat pumps cost-competitive with natural gas, the main heating source in Massachusetts and the United States as a whole. Unfortunately, even though heat pumps are much more efficient than natural gas furnaces, they are often still more expensive to operate because the cost of electricity is just so high in many areas. Before these discounted rates, only about 23% of households would have saved money by switching from natural gas to a heat pump. But with these new rates, about half of homeowners can now expect to save. And with recent price increases in heating oil, it could cause even more homeowners to make the switch. The other cool part of this program is that it seems to be inspiring other states to consider similar legislation, such as Xcel Energy in Minnesota and Central Maine Power in Maine, and also New York and Rhode Island as well.
