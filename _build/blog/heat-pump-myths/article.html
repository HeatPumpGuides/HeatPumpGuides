A lot of what people say about heat pumps is simply wrong. Some of it was true 20 or 30 years ago and hasn't been since. Here are the six myths we hear most often, and what's actually true.

The six myths

  1. Heat pumps don't work in cold weather
  2. Heat pumps are very noisy
  3. Heat pumps only provide heat
  4. Heat pumps need more maintenance
  5. Heat pumps only work in well-insulated homes
  6. Heat pumps aren't environmentally friendly

Myth 1: Heat pumps don't work in cold weather

This is the most common myth. Some people say heat pumps stop working below freezing, 32°F (0°C). That's not true. Modern cold-climate heat pumps keep heating down to −13°F, and some models go to −20°F or below.

To be fair, this one used to be true. Twenty or thirty years ago, many heat pumps couldn't keep up in real cold, and some shut off below freezing. The last decade has brought big improvements, and today's cold-climate models are a different machine.

Efficiency drops in the cold, but stays high

A heat pump's efficiency does fall as the outdoor temperature drops, because it has to work harder to pull heat from colder air. It doesn't stop working, and it stays well above 100% efficient. That's better than any furnace or electric baseboard heater can manage.

Take the Mitsubishi MSZ-FS12NA mini-split spec sheet we looked at in our episode on COP, SCOP and HSPF. It lists a coefficient of performance (COP) of 2.24 at −5°F and 2.08 at −13°F. That's still more than 200% efficiency at temperatures most cold places rarely reach.

The coldest countries in Europe use the most heat pumps

Finland, Norway and Sweden are the three coldest countries in Europe, and they have the three highest heat pump adoption rates on the continent. In Norway, more than 60% of households have a heat pump. Inland Norway regularly hits −20°C (−4°F) in winter.

On the episode we said this Mitsubishi unit has a COP of 2.2 at −13°F. Its spec sheet lists 2.24 at −5°F and 2.08 at −13°F. The point stands: it's still more than twice as efficient as a furnace at −13°F.

Myth 2: Heat pumps are very noisy

A heat pump makes some noise, because the outdoor unit has a fan that blows air across its coils to pull heat from outside. A modern heat pump is about as loud as a quiet refrigerator. It may be slightly louder than a gas furnace, but not by much.

A useful test: if you don't notice your central AC running when you're inside in summer, you won't notice a heat pump running outside in winter. Units keep getting quieter as manufacturers respond to local noise rules. If yours is loud, it probably needs maintenance or is getting old.

Myth 3: Heat pumps only provide heat

It's an understandable mistake, since "heat" is in the name. But a heat pump works in both directions. It heats your home in winter and cools it in summer. A heat pump is really an air conditioner that can also run in reverse. One system handles both jobs.

Myth 4: Heat pumps need more maintenance

A heat pump is an HVAC system, so like any furnace or air conditioner it needs regular care: filter changes, and an occasional tune-up or coil cleaning. That's about it. Heat pumps don't need meaningfully more maintenance than other systems. A typical heat pump lasts 12 to 15 years, and yearly maintenance keeps it efficient for that whole time.

Myth 5: Heat pumps only work in well-insulated homes

Every heating system works better in a well-insulated house, whether it's a heat pump, a furnace or anything else. Upgrading insulation is almost always worth doing, but it isn't required for a heat pump.

What matters most is sizing. A good contractor does a proper Manual J load calculation, which accounts for how much heat your house actually gains and loses. A heat pump sized that way will heat a home even if it isn't especially well insulated. We cover sizing and insulation in more detail in the pros and cons of heat pumps.

Myth 6: Heat pumps aren't environmentally friendly

The argument goes: heat pumps run on electricity, and the grid still runs mostly on fossil fuels, so what's the point? It's true that fossil fuels still generate the majority of US electricity, roughly 55–60% depending on where you live. Even so, a heat pump on that grid produces less CO2 than a gas furnace.

The efficiency math

  • The most efficient combined-cycle gas power plants turn about 60% of the fuel's energy into electricity.
  • A heat pump turns each unit of that electricity into 3 to 4 units of heat (300–400% efficiency), depending on the weather.
  • Put together, burning gas at a power plant to run a heat pump delivers about 1.8 to 2.4 units of heat per unit of gas. A 95%-efficient home furnace delivers about 0.95.

So even if every kilowatt-hour came from a gas plant, a heat pump would release roughly half the CO2 of even a high-efficiency furnace, or less. As the grid adds more wind and solar, the gap widens. It's the same argument people have about electric cars, and the answer is the same: we should keep cleaning up the grid, but the electric option already comes out ahead.
