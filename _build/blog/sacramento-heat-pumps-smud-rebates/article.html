If you live in the Sacramento area and heat your home with gas, SMUD will pay up to $3,000 toward switching to a variable-speed heat pump. Jairo Gonzalez, who owns Hella Comfort Heating & Air and says about 90% of his installs are now heat pumps, joined the podcast as our first guest to explain how the rebate works, what a heat pump actually costs to run, and how to avoid a bad install.

The short answer

  • SMUD rebate: up to $3,000 for replacing gas heat with a variable-speed heat pump, and $1,000 for upgrading an existing heat pump to an inverter model. A SMUD network contractor has to do the install.
  • Gas line: only the furnace's gas line is capped. You can keep a gas stove or water heater.
  • Running cost: inverter heat pumps adjust their output in small steps instead of running flat out, so they use far less electricity than the single-speed systems most homes are replacing.
  • Cold weather: Sacramento doesn't need a cold-climate heat pump. A standard inverter model handles its winters.
  • Biggest mistake: choosing the cheapest bid. Look for heat pump experience, pulled permits, and a contractor who checks your ductwork.

How the SMUD heat pump rebate works

The Sacramento Municipal Utility District (SMUD), the area's electric utility, runs the main heat pump incentive here. Jairo's company is a participating contractor, which matters: only contractors in SMUD's network can get you the rebate.

SMUD heat pump HVAC rebates, as described on the episode
Your upgradeRebate
Gas furnace to a variable-speed (inverter) heat pumpup to $3,000
Existing standard heat pump to an inverter heat pump$1,000
Heat pump added alongside a gas furnace (dual fuel)not eligible

Amounts match SMUD's February 2026 rebate announcement. Two-stage systems qualify for a smaller conversion rebate, and SMUD also offers a separate bonus toward electrical panel and circuit upgrades when you convert from gas. Confirm current amounts with SMUD before you sign a contract.

Do you have to cap your gas line?

Only for heating. To qualify, the contractor caps the gas line that fed your furnace, so you can no longer heat the house with gas. Everything else stays: Jairo was clear that you can keep cooking on a gas stove and keep a gas water heater. The rebate covers the HVAC system only.

Who handles the paperwork?

The contractor does. Jairo pays a third-party company that handles his permits and SMUD paperwork and schedules the city and program inspections. The homeowner doesn't have to file anything. The rebate usually arrives within a month to six weeks, once every inspection has passed. In some cases his company fronts the rebate to the customer up front and collects it from SMUD afterwards.

What about state and federal incentives?

For now, the SMUD rebate is the one doing the work in Sacramento. The two other programs Jairo mentioned are not available:

  • Federal tax credit (25C): the heat pump tax credit of up to $2,000 a year ended for equipment placed in service after December 31, 2025.
  • TECH Clean California: the statewide program's single-family heat pump incentives are fully reserved and not accepting new reservations. Jairo expects it to return if the state refills the funding.

The California heat pump page lists statewide programs and their current status.

Heat pump vs gas: what it costs to run in Sacramento

Sacramento homeowners pay twice: PG&E gas in winter, which Jairo says gets pricey, and high electricity bills for air conditioning in summer. A heat pump replaces both the furnace and the AC. Since a heat pump is an air conditioner that also runs in reverse, the summer savings count just as much as the winter ones.

The key difference is how the compressor runs. Most systems Jairo replaces are single-speed: they are either off or running at 100%. An inverter (variable-speed) heat pump ramps up and down in roughly 1% steps, and some can go above 100% when they need to. Most of the time a house only needs to hold its temperature within a degree or two, so the system runs at a low, steady output instead of cycling at full power.

How to estimate the difference yourself

Jairo's rule of thumb is to look at how many amps a system draws. Electricity is billed per kilowatt-hour, so the math goes:

  1. Volts × amps ÷ 1,000 = kilowatts the system draws while running.
  2. Kilowatts × hours running = kilowatt-hours (kWh) used.
  3. kWh × your rate = cost.

As an illustration only: a single-speed unit drawing 15 amps at 240 volts uses 3.6 kW. Running 6 hours on a hot day, that's 21.6 kWh. An inverter system holding the same house at a third of that draw for 10 hours uses 12 kWh, about 45% less, even though it ran longer. Your real numbers depend on the equipment, your home and your SMUD rate plan.

Why a heat pump runs longer, and why that's normal

New heat pump owners often worry that the system never seems to turn off. That's by design. A gas furnace blasts air at roughly 130–140°F, overheats the room, shuts off, and kicks back on once you get cold. An inverter heat pump delivers air closer to 100–130°F and warms up gradually, heating the whole space evenly and running for longer stretches. Jairo finds that homeowners are happy with it once someone explains the difference.

You may also notice a brief puff of cool air in winter. That's the defrost cycle, when the outdoor unit clears frost off its coil. Many models delay the indoor fan during defrost so you barely feel it, and it only lasts a short time.

Do you need a cold-climate heat pump in Sacramento?

No. Sacramento winters are mild, and Jairo says a standard variable-speed inverter heat pump does the job across the area. The units he installs keep heating down to around -3 to -5°F, depending on the model, far colder than Sacramento gets. Efficiency does drop as the outdoor temperature falls, but at Sacramento's winter temperatures a regular inverter heat pump still saves money. The old idea that heat pumps don't work in the cold doesn't apply here.

Ducted heat pump or ductless mini-split?

Jairo says the choice comes down to what you want the system to do and how you feel about seeing equipment on your walls.

Wall-mounted mini-splitsHomes without ducts, or rooms you want to heat and cool separatelyA remote for each unit

If you want a ducted system, the contractor needs to check attic clearances first to see what will fit. Inside the house, the air handler (sometimes called a fan coil) goes where the furnace used to be. The outdoor unit holds the inverter compressor.

What about keeping the furnace (dual fuel)?

A dual fuel setup pairs a heat pump with your existing gas furnace. You get a very efficient air conditioner and heat pump, with gas as backup. The catch in Sacramento: dual fuel installs don't qualify for SMUD's conversion rebate, because the gas heat stays connected.

Heat pump install mistakes to avoid

Jairo's biggest piece of advice applies wherever you live: hire someone who has actually installed heat pumps. Many contractors still don't install them, and some still believe they're less efficient than gas, because they haven't kept up with the equipment. Before you sign, check that your contractor:

  • has heat pump experience with the type of system you want (ducted, mini-split or central)
  • pulls permits for the job
  • measures and checks your ductwork, supply and return, so airflow is right for the new system
  • takes care of the whole process instead of cutting steps to hit a lower price

Most of the problems Jairo is called out to fix come from installs where the homeowner chose the cheapest bid and steps got skipped. The result is a system that never reaches its rated efficiency, plus callbacks and repairs that cost more than doing it right the first time. Maintenance tips that keep a heat pump efficient

  • Change the filter every 2–3 months, or monthly if the system runs a lot. A clean filter means less work for the system and cleaner air. You don't need a premium filter: a cheaper one changed more often works.
  • Watch the filter during wildfire smoke. Filters clog much faster when the air is smoky, which is common in California.
  • Keep the outdoor coil clear of leaves and debris, if you're comfortable doing it.
  • Get a professional check-up every 6 to 12 months to keep the system running to the manufacturer's specs.

Filters matter more than most people think. Jairo described a recent service call where the tenant didn't know the system had a filter. It was so clogged that the unit overheated and shut itself off after five minutes of running.
